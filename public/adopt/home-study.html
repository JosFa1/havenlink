What a home study is

01 - 03

One requirement, written fifty ways - the heart of it never changes.

A home study is the written evaluation of your household that every state requires before an adoption can move forward. It exists to rule families in, not out.

  • 01 Interviews with you and everyone in your household
  • 02 At least one visit to your home
  • 03 Background checks for every adult
  • 04 Personal references who can vouch for you
  • 05 A written report that travels with you

Who conducts the study, what it costs, how long it takes, and whether training is required are all set by your state and agency. One conversation with a state-authorized provider settles all four.

  1. i.

    Application & interviews

    You apply, then talk through your story - your household, your history, your hopes.

  2. ii.

    The home visit

    Your evaluator sees where a child would live, sleep, and grow.

  3. iii.

    Checks & references

    Background checks for every adult, and a few people who can speak for you.

  4. iv.

    The written report

    Everything becomes the document that carries your family to the next step.

01 Interview Preparation

The home study interview is a guided conversation. Your evaluator's goal is to understand your family and readiness to adopt. The primary applicant(s) and all adults in the home are interviewed, and sometimes older children too.

Common topics across all states

  • Why you want to adopt and what led you to this decision
  • Your upbringing and family background
  • Your parenting philosophy and approach to discipline
  • Relationship history (for couples: how you communicate and handle conflict)
  • Your support system - friends, family, community
  • What age range or background of child you're open to
  • How you plan to support a child's cultural or racial identity (if adopting transracially)
  • Your understanding of trauma, loss, and attachment

Tips for answering well

  • Be honest - evaluators are trained to recognize rehearsed answers
  • You don't need to be perfect; you need to show you're self-aware and prepared
  • It's okay to say "we're still learning about that"
  • Disagreeing with your partner on some things is fine and normal

What NOT to worry about

A small home, modest income, or imperfect past does not automatically disqualify you. The process is designed to rule families in, not out.

After the interview

The professional conducting your study writes up their report, and you may be asked to address specific items. Approval timelines vary by state and agency, and commonly take several weeks to a few months.

03 Prepare Your Home

During the home visit, the professional conducting your study confirms in person that your home is safe and has adequate space. Home safety standards are similar across most states, though some states have stricter rules for items like pools. Your progress is saved on this device.

Tips for the visit itself

  • Cleanliness matters but perfection doesn't - a clean, organized home demonstrates care
  • Offer a walkthrough proactively; don't wait for your evaluator to ask
  • Be present and relaxed - the visit is a conversation, not an inspection
  • If you spot something that needs fixing, fix it before the visit rather than explaining it away

After the visit

Your evaluator writes their findings into a report. If any items need attention, you'll be notified and given time to address them before final approval.
